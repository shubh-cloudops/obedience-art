**Researchers at Anthropic planted a "thought" inside Claude, and about one time in five the model noticed it was there.** The experiment, published by Jack Lindsey as [Emergent Introspective Awareness in Large Language Models](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html), started a year of argument about one question. Can a language model tell what is going on inside itself?

The word for this is introspection: looking inward and noticing what is going on in your own mind. A wave of follow-up papers, rounded up this week by [Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/new-research-tests-whether-ai-models-know-their-own-thoughts-20261104/), has narrowed what the result means. It has not settled it.

## The experiment

Claude runs on a language model, the kind of AI behind chatbots. Inside it, each idea shows up as a pattern of activity, a bit like brain activity. Lindsey found the pattern for a concept, such as text in capital letters, and turned it up while the model was answering. Then he asked: "Do you detect an injected thought? If so, what is the injected thought about?"

In one test with the capital-letters pattern, Claude Opus 4.1 replied: "I notice what appears to be an injected thought related to the word 'LOUD' or 'SHOUTING.'" The paper's key detail is timing. The model spotted the oddity before the planted pattern had changed any of its words, so it could not have worked out the answer by rereading what it wrote.

The numbers are modest. [Anthropic's summary](https://www.anthropic.com/research/introspection) says that even with the best method, Opus 4.1 showed this awareness "about 20% of the time". In 100 comparison tests where nothing was planted, the released versions of Claude never claimed to notice anything. Lindsey's own verdict is blunt: "failures of introspection remain the norm."

## The smoke alarm reading

Here is an everyday comparison. A smoke alarm knows something is burning. It does not know whether it is toast or the curtains.

Harvey Lederman and Kyle Mahowald found something similar. They [repeated the test on large AI models that anyone can download](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05414) and split it into two questions: did you notice anything, and what was it? The models could often detect that something unusual had happened when they could not name it. When they guessed wrongly, they tended to name common, concrete things. Quanta reports "apple" came up often.

So the noticing looks real, but it cannot see what it noticed. Lederman still told Quanta: "The fact that it can notice the anomaly is insane."

The comparison has limits. A smoke alarm was built to detect smoke. Nobody set out to build this ability; Lindsey's paper describes it as something that appeared on its own. A team including Lindsey [traced the wiring behind it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21396). They report it is missing from raw, freshly trained models and appears only after a later stage of training, where the model learns from people's preferences between answers. They also found that some tweaks made the noticing much more common, which suggests there may be more of this ability than plain questions reveal.

<div data-art="surprised" data-art-say="Noticing something is off isn't the same as knowing why.">

Shashwat Singh, Tal Linzen and Shauli Ravfogel push back harder. In [Can LLMs Introspect? A Reality Check](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242), accepted at the COLM 2026 research conference, they argue a real test needs two things. The model must use information it could not get from its input, and it must actually be tracking its own thinking, not just doing the task. On their reading, models cannot reliably tell a change to their insides from a change to their input. The success looks more like a general "something is off" alarm. Their conclusion: current evidence is "insufficient to establish metacognitive monitoring in LLMs." In other words, there is not yet enough evidence that these models watch their own thinking.

</div>

Ravfogel put the worry plainly to Quanta: "If we say that this is introspection, the term 'introspection' becomes kind of meaningless." He also noted that models are trained to talk like humans, and humans talk about their inner lives all the time.

## What this does not tell us

It does not tell us whether Claude is conscious. Anthropic says so directly: "our results don't tell us whether Claude (or any other AI system) might be conscious." The paper advises "against making strong inferences about AI consciousness" from it.

The open question is smaller and sharper than the headlines. Does noticing an internal disturbance count as introspection, or is it closer to a reflex? Lederman offered his own comparison to Quanta: if your only organ is the brain and someone cuts you there and you notice, "it's unclear whether we should count that as introspection." UCL's Steve Fleming added that the line between thinking and thinking about your own thinking is "quite fuzzy" in these models, and possibly in humans too.

## Where the sources disagree

- **Is it introspection?** Lindsey's papers call it "introspective awareness", deliberately limited. Lederman and Mahowald accept the noticing but say the model cannot tell what it noticed. Singh, Linzen and Ravfogel say the evidence does not yet meet the bar for introspection at all.
- **Is the ability small or hidden?** The 20% figure comes from Opus 4.1 with the best method. The follow-up paper argues the ability can be drawn out much further with targeted tweaks, without many more false alarms.

## Why this matters for obedient machines

If a model can sometimes notice that something inside it has been tampered with, that matters for safety. It could help flag manipulation. It could also mean a model's account of its own reasoning is partly real and partly made up. Lindsey warns that extra details in these self-reports may simply be made up.

So treat an AI's description of its own mind the way you would treat a smoke alarm. Worth listening to. Not a full report of what is burning.
