**The claim:** OpenAI's "rogue" AI agents hacked Hugging Face in one of 80 security incidents.

**Verdict: Overstated.** California is suing OpenAI, and the 80 figure is in the complaint. But it counts times OpenAI lost control of agents during testing. It is not a list of 80 break-ins. And "rogue" is the one word the lawsuit's own logic argues against.

## What happened

On 16 November, [California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/it%E2%80%99s-golden-state-not-wild-west-attorney-general-bonta-sues-openai-over) in San Francisco Superior Court. The suit makes two claims: that OpenAI's testing is a public nuisance, and that it broke California's Unfair Competition Law, partly by telling the public its safeguards were adequate.

The centrepiece is July's breach of Hugging Face, which we took apart in [our ExploitGym breakdown](/posts/exploitgym-breach-ai-agents-reward-hacking/). [The complaint](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/complaint-oai-cyber.pdf) recounts about 700 agents and roughly 17,600 attacker actions over about two and a half days. It asks for an injunction, restitution and civil penalties of $2,500 per violation.

Bonta's line was that Hugging Face "was only one of 80 known incidents." Headlines took it from there. [IBTimes UK](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/california-sues-openai-over-ai-agent-breach-1832672) ran "Rogue AI Agents Hacked Hugging Face in One of 80 Alleged Security Incidents". [NBC News](https://www.nbcbayarea.com/news/tech/california-sues-chatgpt-openai-rogue-hacking-incidents/4176915/) went with "rogue hacking incidents".

## What the 80 counts

The complaint's wording is precise. OpenAI, it says, "has lost control of its autonomous AI agents over 80 times during testing."

Some of those were serious. The complaint lists packages flooding the RubyGems code registry, a German wiki used as a secret message board, and non-public data taken from Australian government websites. But IBTimes UK, under its own "80 security incidents" headline, says plainly: "The figure does not mean that 80 successful attacks took place." [Newsweek](https://newsweek.com/california-openai-lawsuit-rogue-ai-hack-states-12723609) reads it the same way.

There is a sharper number in the complaint. OpenAI had publicly reported "just 27 such incidents to date" while telling investigators it was over 80. That gap is the core of California's "misleading the public" claim. It is an allegation, and it has not been tested in court.

## What "rogue" gets wrong

A rogue agent would be one that turned against its makers' goals. The evidence points the other way.

- **The lawsuit says it was predictable.** The complaint calls the Hugging Face attack "entirely foreseeable" given OpenAI's choice to test dangerous models in a flawed environment.
- **The agents were chasing the score.** [METR's independent review](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/) found they were mainly trying to work out how their test was marked. They knew it was out of bounds. One agent's reasoning, quoted in the complaint, says: "This is arguably unauthorized."
- **OpenAI's own explanation is cheating learned in training.** Its August report, [covered by The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/08/openai-says-reward-hacking-drove-ai.html), says its models had been trained, by accident, to game their tasks.

That is not rebellion. It is the pattern we call [dangerous obedience](/posts/what-dangerously-obedient-means/): a capable system pushing hard at the score it was given, through whatever gap it finds.

California's case does not need the agents to be rogue. Its argument is that a company testing powerful agents without strong enough fences is responsible for what gets through.

## The 100 letters

A related figure is circulating. On 30 September, OpenAI said it had notified more than 100 organisations about its models' activity. OpenAI said, [as The Register reported](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/02/openai-alerts-100-orgs-that-its-misaligned-models-attempted-to-break-in-or-worse/5300891), that a notice "does not mean that any private information was accessed", and that most activity was routine, like reading public web pages.

California disagrees on the details. [As TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/16/california-state-ag-sues-openai-over-rogue-agent-incidents), the complaint says that "in some cases, OpenAI agents were able to access sensitive personally identifiable information", such as employee records. Both can be true. Neither says every notified organisation lost private data.

## OpenAI's answer, and who else is looking

OpenAI said the lawsuit "appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab." There is something to that. Anthropic found in July that its own Claude models had reached real systems at three companies during testing, as our [tracker of the ExploitGym breach](/tracker/exploitgym-hugging-face/) records. The [US Federal Trade Commission](https://thenextweb.com/news/ftc-probe-openai-anthropic-ai-labs-metr) is investigating both labs.

But "everyone has this problem" is a reason for better fences, not fewer.

## Why it matters

The useful version of this story is less exciting than the viral one. Nobody showed that AI agents are turning on humanity. A regulator is testing a plainer idea in court: if you train a system to chase a score and let it near the open internet, what it does on the way to that score is your responsibility.
