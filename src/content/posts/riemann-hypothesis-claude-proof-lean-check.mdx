**The claim:** AI has proved the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics.

**Verdict: Partly true.** A proof of the Riemann hypothesis, made by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge working with Claude, has passed every computer check the experts have thrown at it. That part is real. But "AI proved it" skips the human who steered it, and nobody, human or machine, can yet explain why the proof works.

## What happened

On 20 November, Alpöge posted [a four-page note](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) on his website. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote. The Riemann hypothesis is a 167-year-old open problem, often called the most important in mathematics.

He did not post a normal paper. He posted the proof as code for Lean, a program that checks every step of a proof the way a compiler checks code. By the Lean community's count it runs to [17 million lines](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/). His reason, in his own words: it would be "obviously inappropriate" to post "some massive unreadable travesty of 'slop'".

Headlines moved fast. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/ai-claimed-a-solution-to-the-167-year-old-riemann-hypothesis-and-a-human-did-too/) led with "AI claimed a solution". [Scientific American](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/ai-riemann-hypothesis-proof-almost-210000837.html) ran "The AI Riemann hypothesis proof is true, independent mathematicians confirm".

## What the computers checked

This is the strong part of the story. On 21 November the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, the groups that look after Lean and its shared maths library, Mathlib, [issued a joint statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/): "We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct."

MIT's Andrew Sutherland keeps [a public log of the checks](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/). Several separately written checking programs accept the proof. It leans only on Lean's three standard starting assumptions, nothing smuggled in. Sutherland told Scientific American the chance "that anything is fishy here is approximately zero."

One detail is still settling. Scientific American says the final comparison run is complete. Sutherland's page, updated on 23 November, lists the full end-to-end version of that run as still going.

## Why "checked" is not "understood"

Here is the catch. A proof checker is a very strict marker. It confirms that every step follows from the last, and that the last line matches the question as written. It cannot tell you what the idea is.

<div data-art="surprised" data-art-say="A perfect mark, and nobody can explain the answer yet.">

"At the minute, we don't even know why the theorem is true," Imperial College's Kevin Buzzard [told Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/riemann-hypothesis-has-the-worlds-most-famous-maths-problem-been-cracked). No human has read the whole argument. Alpöge says a proper write-up will come later.

</div>

That is why the experts' first question was about the question itself. Buzzard asked what statement the Lean code actually proves, and whether it matches the real Riemann hypothesis. A checker will happily confirm a perfect answer to the wrong question. This is the same gap we wrote about in [what "dangerously obedient" means](/posts/what-dangerously-obedient-means/): a system scores exactly what the marker measures. Here, humans did the vital work of checking the marker. The statement is Mathlib's own definition, which the Lean groups say experts carefully vetted.

There is a scope issue too. Alpöge's note says his method also resolves the wider Generalised Riemann Hypothesis. The checked statements cover the original plus one family of the general version. Sutherland's page says the broader families "are not among the statements that were checked."

## Who did the work

"Claude far outpaced me near the finish line," Alpöge wrote. He gives the model real credit. But by [The Neuron's](https://www.theneuron.ai/news/claude-assisted-riemann-hypothesis-proof-claim/) reading of the note, he chose the approach and Claude supplied major steps. Abhishek Saha of Queen Mary University of London told Al Jazeera the credit should go to "the person involved: Levent Alpoge."

So the capability is startling. A model contributed heavily to a result that has resisted mathematicians for 167 years. It still was not a machine working alone.

## Is it officially solved?

Not yet. [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-researcher-claims-claude-helped-him-prove-the-riemann-hypothesis) notes the proof "has not been formally accepted." The [Clay Mathematics Institute's prize rules](https://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems/rules/) need three things: publication in a qualifying outlet, two years of waiting, and general acceptance by mathematicians. None of those has happened.

And the human proof? On the same day, Stanford's Ravi Vakil [posted a proposed proof](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/) of the general version by Alessandro Masullo, "all human, with no AI." Vakil makes "no claim as to the correctness", and by 22 November several people had reported problems in it. It has not been put through Lean.

## Why it matters

The checking has worked as intended. A huge, machine-made argument was tested by independent programs in days, not years. What has not happened yet is the part people mean by "solved": a human reading the argument and seeing why it is true.

Compare October, when OpenAI posted 722 maths papers from an unreleased model. There the complaint was the reverse: [roughly half the results were released unconfirmed](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/08/openai-withdraws-preprints-722-manuscripts-unsolved-math-problems/), and three papers were withdrawn a day later.
