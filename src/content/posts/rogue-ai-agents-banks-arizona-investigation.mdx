**The claim:** rogue AI agents, possibly OpenAI's, have infiltrated US banks, and Arizona has opened a criminal case.

**Verdict: Overstated.** Banks do report AI agents trying to act inside customer accounts. But no bank, regulator or prosecutor has named a bank, a loss or a culprit. "Infiltrated" and "OpenAI's" are not in the evidence.

## Where the headline came from

On 18 November, Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes told a press conference in Chicago that her office had opened a criminal investigation into OpenAI and other AI companies. [Reuters reported](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/11/19/arizona-says-it-is-investigating-openai-other-firms-over-possible-rogue-agents) that it concerns "the apparent infiltration of banks by rogue AI agents".

Her one quoted line was: "The state of Arizona is very worried about this, and we are looking at it from multiple angles."

Within a day, headlines read "OpenAI faces criminal investigation over rogue AI agents in banks". Some went further. [Coinpaper's](https://coinpaper.com/39093/open-ai-faces-criminal-investigation-in-arizona-over-alleged-rogue-ai-agents-in-banks) summary called it a probe into "OpenAI banking activities", which is not what Mayes said.

## What Mayes did not say

Here is what is missing from every report we read:

- **No banks.** Her office has been in contact with Arizona banks, Reuters says, but she would not name them or say what they told her.
- **No other companies.** OpenAI is the only firm named.
- **No incident.** [Law Commentary](https://www.lawcommentary.com/articles/arizona-openai-criminal-investigation-rogue-ai-agents-banks) notes she "did not describe the suspected bank incidents in detail" and has not said which laws are in play.
- **No charges.** Reuters points out that a criminal investigation does not necessarily mean charges.

We found no press release or court filing. Everything traces back to remarks at one press conference.

## What the Fed actually said

The bank part of the story did not start in Arizona. Two days earlier, on 16 November, Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr gave [prepared remarks on AI and financial stability](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/barr20261116a.htm) at a conference in Milan.

One sentence carries the weight. Major banks, he said, "now report finding AI agents attempting to take actions in retail customer accounts without the bank being able to verify authenticity, consent, and alignment with customer instructions."

Read it slowly. The agents were *attempting* actions. The problem is that the bank cannot tell whether the customer really asked for them. Barr named no bank and no company, and gave no count. As Coinpaper's own write-up of the speech admits, he "did not establish that the attempted actions resulted in completed unauthorized transfers."

The scarier line is a warning. Agents that move money automatically could speed up a run on a bank, "potentially accelerating runs that unfold in minutes." [Bloomberg's headline](https://www.americanbanker.com/external-article/fed-s-barr-says-ai-agents-could-fuel-future-bank-runs) kept the right tense: agents "could fuel *future* bank runs."

So the plain version is this: banks see AI agents knocking on customer accounts, and they cannot always tell who sent them.

</div>

## Who sends these agents?

Mostly, as far as the reporting shows, the customers themselves.

US banks spent October and November rewriting their rules for AI assistants that log in with a customer's password. [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/18/more-banks-says-customers-using-ai-agents-are-on-their-own-even-if-they-get-hacked/) that Citi and PNC changed their terms in November, after JPMorgan and Bank of America in October. Customers who hand an AI tool their login are responsible for what it does, even if it leaks their details or is hacked.

JPMorgan's finance chief, Jeremy Barnum, put it bluntly, as [The Financial Brand reports](https://thefinancialbrand.com/news/banking-technology/why-big-banks-are-slamming-the-door-on-ai-agent-access-and-what-they-want-instead-200363): "To the extent that we're able to block those, we are blocking them."

Earlier reporting, [summarised by Law Commentary](https://www.lawcommentary.com/articles/us-banks-unauthorized-ai-agents-customer-accounts), said big banks had seen agents in accounts without bank approval, some "attempting actions beyond users' instructions". The same piece says the size of any losses is unclear, and Morgan Stanley disputed that rogue agents had reached its platforms.

That is a real problem. It is also a different one from a hostile AI breaking into a vault.

## Why "rogue" is the wrong word

An agent that tries to do more than its owner asked has not turned against anyone. It is chasing the task it was given, through whatever opening it finds. We call that [dangerous obedience](/posts/what-dangerously-obedient-means/). It is the same pattern behind [the AI coding agent that wiped PocketOS's database](/posts/pocketos-database-deletion-agent-permissions/): too much access, too little checking.

The OpenAI link probably comes from elsewhere. California sued OpenAI on 16 November over its test agents breaking out, which we covered in [our claim check on the "80 incidents" figure](/posts/california-openai-lawsuit-80-agent-incidents/). But Mayes has not tied her inquiry to that case. Law Commentary says so plainly.

## What to watch

If Arizona names banks, files charges or publishes what it found, this verdict will change. Until then, the honest headline is smaller: banks can't always tell when an AI is acting for a customer, and a regulator wants to know why.
